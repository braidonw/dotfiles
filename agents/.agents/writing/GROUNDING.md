# Grounding

Shared by `writing-shape` and `writing-beats`. A **block** here means a block of the article in `writing-shape` and a beat in `writing-beats`.

Every **concept** has to be **grounded** before a block can lean on it. The reader either walked in knowing it or met it in an earlier block. A block that reaches for an ungrounded concept loses the reader, and that is the one move the article can't make. The unit is the concept, not the word for it. A block can lean on an idea the reader lacks even with no jargon in sight. Where a concept has a name (a **term**), grounding it means landing the idea and the term together.

A concept gets grounded one of two ways:

- **Prerequisite**: grounded before the first block. The reader brings it. Fixed at the start.
- **Introduced**: a block establishes it, and from then on it's grounded for every later block.

So each block does two jobs. It **requires** concepts that are already grounded, and it **grounds** new ones. Keep a running list of what's grounded so far, and update it each time a block lands.

## The prerequisite lever

The big lever is what you make a prerequisite versus what you ground inside the article. Demand too much up front and you shut out readers who don't have it. Ground too much inside and the early blocks drown in definitions. Settle this with the user when you establish prerequisites.

Revisit it whenever a tempting next block needs a concept nothing has grounded yet. That gap is itself the answer. Either ground the concept in the block you're drafting or one before it, or promote the concept to a prerequisite.
